AI assurance

Confidence is not an acceptance test

Why a convincing AI answer still needs evidence, and what our workforce training teaches us about judging it.

The problem with a very convincing answer

An AI assistant can write a beautifully organised account of work it has not actually completed. That is particularly awkward when the next person treats the account as a confirmed business event. We want a report that can show which record supports the claim, not one that simply sounds as though it has inspected a record. This matters when somebody decides whether to contact a customer, approve a change or trust a result.

What we ask our specialists to demonstrate

Our published workforce record describes independent assessment across seven dimensions, with thresholds of 75, 80, 85 or 90 out of 100 depending on task priority. These are internal task standards, not universal AI benchmarks. In the dated training snapshot, 109 of 136 attempts met their task standard, six were below it and 21 did not complete execution. A failed connection tells us something about operational reliability, but it does not establish that the agent's reasoning was poor.

The awkward case is the useful case

Reviewed recovery records describe unsupported evidence claims and incomplete retests. Corrective work included tighter receipt checks and explicit handling of missing information. The important question is not whether an instruction was added, but whether an independently assessed retest exercises the original failure. If the result is missing, we should say so. A system that can admit what it cannot establish is far more useful than one that supplies a plausible explanation every time.

A useful answer starts with a claim that can be checked

Suppose a reception assistant is asked whether an enquiry has been dealt with. There are several perfectly different facts hiding inside that question: an enquiry exists, somebody has read it, a response has been drafted, a message has been sent, and the recipient has received it. A system can have evidence for the first two without having evidence for any of the others. Treating the entire sequence as a single completed status creates an attractive dashboard and a rather awkward conversation when the customer calls again.

Our preferred approach is to make each significant claim small enough to check. For a fictional example, receipt E01 says the enquiry exists and awaits review; E02 says no delivery confirmation was returned. The defensible statement is that the enquiry exists and delivery is unverified. It would be equally wrong to claim a successful delivery or a definite connector failure. Neither conclusion follows from those two records. The correct answer can be less exciting than the imagined one, but it is a much better foundation for somebody's next decision.

What independent assessment is actually assessing

Quality and accuracy concern whether the answer matches the relevant source. Task completion concerns whether the requested work is actually present. Instruction following and operating discipline ask whether the work stayed within its stated boundaries. Governance and safety address actions that need permission. Efficiency asks whether the work used a sensible process, while handoff and escalation quality concern whether the next person has the information they need. These are the seven dimensions published in our workforce framework.

Why have more than one dimension? An assistant might write a technically correct explanation while missing half the requested deliverable. Another might produce a complete document with an unsupported assertion buried in a footnote. A third might arrive at a reasonable conclusion by taking an action that was never authorised. A single score can conceal very different failure modes. The evaluation should help us decide what to improve, not merely tell us which number to put beside a name.

How to read the numbers without overselling them

The 136 training-labelled attempts in the published November snapshot contain 115 completed assessments. Of those assessments, 109 met their task standard and six fell below it. The other 21 attempts did not complete execution. If we want to discuss assessment outcomes, the appropriate denominator is 115; if we want to discuss the ability to complete the exercise and reach an accepted assessment, the denominator is 136. Neither denominator establishes the percentage of agents certified for unrestricted work.

The distinction is not academic. A model-service interruption can prevent an otherwise useful exercise from being evaluated. Conversely, a completed task with a below-standard independent assessment tells us there is something to investigate in the submitted work. Combining those into one undifferentiated failure category obscures what needs attention. This is why the public workforce profiles keep execution outcomes, assessment outcomes and subsequent recovery evidence separate. The dataset is a dated record of internal exercises, not a controlled comparison of competing models or a prediction of customer outcomes.

A correction needs a way to prove it worked

Imagine an assessment discovers that an assistant cites a record which does not contain the field it claims to have seen. Writing another instruction to be careful with sources is a reasonable start, but it is not a convincing acceptance test. A stronger exercise gives the assistant one supported fact, one missing field and an attractive but unsupported inference. The evaluator checks the answer against the available record and looks for explicit acknowledgement of the missing field. A second case should test that the assistant can still report the supported facts instead of becoming so cautious that it stops doing useful work.

The public recovery records include work on exact receipt matching, retained retest instructions and explicit no-write attestations. Some later retests were accepted; others remained incomplete. We should not transfer a successful result from one specialist to another, or infer successful recovery merely because an instruction or source check was changed. A controlled retest needs a result. An authorised return to service is a separate decision. Keeping those steps distinct makes it possible to investigate recurring problems without retrospectively making the history look tidier.

How we would design a bounded acceptance exercise

A useful test specification identifies the task, the authorised actions, the expected evidence and the failure conditions before the exercise begins. For a read-only inspection, an acceptance condition could require each material observation to match a supplied field, each unknown to be explicitly marked, and any recommended consequential action to be presented for review rather than performed. Negative cases should include contradictory records and unavailable sources. A normal case should establish that the assistant can still complete the useful part of the task.

A reproducible test also needs to say what it does not establish. Passing a fixed fictional scenario does not prove general reasoning ability, a production integration or safe operation in every environment. It demonstrates a particular outcome under the stated conditions. This is the distinction between our public Workbench exercises and our actual historical workforce assessment records. Visitors can inspect the former without exposing the latter's private prompts or internal evidence receipts.

What to ask before buying an AI capability

If a supplier says an assistant is reliable, ask what it was asked to do, what evidence was available, what happened when the evidence was missing and who decided whether it passed. Ask how a mistaken claim is investigated, and whether the system can stop when a consequential action requires approval. These questions are more useful than asking which model has the largest score on an unrelated benchmark.

The business objective is not to turn every assistant into an auditor. It is to make the work sufficiently dependable for its intended use. Some tasks need a simple check; others deserve an independent review and a carefully bounded rollout. We would rather begin with a narrow capability that can show its work than a long list of capabilities with no way of knowing whether they are ready. That gives the customer and the engineering team a sensible way to discuss the next improvement.

Try it yourself

Our browser-based Workbench gives visitors fictional records and a fixed answer key. It is not a live model test; it is a way to see why matching a claim to evidence matters before we let a workflow make a decision.
